I owned mobile for Pulse, an offline-first inspection app for auditors, on Android and iOS, from an early
prototype to 100+ B2B enterprise clients. For most of that time it was two native apps: Kotlin on Android,
Swift on iOS, kept in feature parity. In 2025 I led a full rewrite of both into a single React Native
codebase. I architected it and built it alongside the team, and we completed it in six months, before I
left in May 2025.

This isn't a "native versus React Native" post. The native apps worked. The earlier posts on
[Pulse's sync engine](/writing/offline-first-sync-lessons) and on
[a local-only app](/writing/offline-first-without-a-server) cover why they were built the way they were.
This one is about the rewrite: why we did it, what we kept, and how it reached users.

## Why rewrite two apps that worked

Keeping two native apps in feature parity has a simple cost: every feature is built twice, once in Kotlin
and once in Swift, and every fix has to land in both. That was the reason for the rewrite, in two parts:

- **One codebase to maintain** instead of two.
- **Shipping faster**, because a feature or a fix reaches Android and iOS at the same time.

Neither reason says the native apps were bad. They say that keeping two of them had a cost, and one
codebase removes it.

## Replicate the behaviour, don't redesign it

The native apps' behaviour had years of field use behind it: records that get a temporary local ID and are
remapped to a server ID when they sync, photos uploaded before the form that references them, upload
progress the auditor can see, and inspection forms built dynamically. So the React Native app replicated
that behaviour rather than redesigning it.

The alternative is harder than it looks. A rewrite that also changes how sync works is two projects at once, and every
difference between the old app and the new one is something to explain to an auditor in the field.
Replicating proven behaviour keeps the rewrite to one question: does the new app do what the old one did?

## I built the core, the team built on top

I set the architecture and built the core myself: sync, offline storage and uploads. The team built the
screens and features on top of it.

In an offline-first app, that core is where the risk sits. Everything on screen depends on the core behaving the way the native
apps did, and in an offline-first app the core is where data gets lost if something is wrong. Screens are
easier to see, test and fix; a sync bug can hide until an auditor's work goes missing.

## Rolling out gradually

The new app didn't replace the native ones for everyone at once. It rolled out gradually, with the native
app still available while users moved over.

For an offline-first app that isn't caution for its own sake. Auditors work without signal, so at any
moment someone may have work on the phone that hasn't reached the server yet. A gradual rollout means a
problem in the new app reaches a few users rather than all of them, and there is still a working app to
fall back on while it's fixed.

## What I'd tell another team

If you're rewriting an offline-first app, roll it out gradually. Keep the old app available and move users
over in stages. A big-bang switch bets every auditor's unsynced work on the new app being right on day one.
