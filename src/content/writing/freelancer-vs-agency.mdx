I'm an independent mobile engineer, so you know which side of this I'm on. But I'd rather win your
project with an argument than with a pitch, so here it is, including the one case where I'd tell you to
hire an agency.

First, the word "freelancer" asks the wrong question. It describes how someone is employed, not how good
they are. The real question is this: **who will make the decisions about your app, and will that person
still be there when those decisions matter?** For most first releases, the best answer is one senior
engineer who owns the app from the first call to the store.

## What an agency fee pays for

An agency quote covers more than the code. It pays for sales, account management and project
management, and for a team whose members can change during your project. Some of that is useful
coordination. A lot of it is the cost of running an agency, not the cost of building your app.

That structure has three side effects worth knowing about before you sign:

- **The person who sells the project often isn't the person who builds it.** The senior people are on
  the sales call. Who writes the code is decided after you've signed.
- **Context gets lost in the handoffs.** What you explain to a salesperson goes to a project manager,
  and then to a developer. Every handoff loses some detail, and the detail is usually what matters.
- **Your app is one of many.** Developers move between projects, and the one who understood your app
  best can be moved to another client halfway through.

None of this makes agencies bad. It's simply how the model works. But it means a lot of your budget goes
into keeping a team coordinated, rather than into your app.

## What one senior engineer gives you instead

- **One person, start to finish.** The engineer on the first call is the one who designs the data model,
  writes the code and handles the store release. Nothing gets lost, because there are no handoffs.
- **Decisions made by the person who lives with them.** If I choose an architecture, I'm the one who has
  to make it work at the next release. That's a strong reason to choose well.
- **Your budget goes into the work.** You pay for engineering, not for layers of coordination.
- **Experience you can't get from a general team.** An app that must work offline, share code between
  Android and iOS, or be rescued from a codebase nobody wants to touch needs someone who has done it
  before.

I've owned apps this way across 13 years on Android and iOS. A few of them:

- **HelperBook**, a household payroll app I built from the first commit to a public release on Google
  Play: product, architecture, UI, Hindi localisation and store submission.
- **A video-first hiring platform**, where I built 100% of the Android client from concept to production
  release: 30+ screens across the candidate and recruiter flows.
- **Pulse**, an inspection platform I owned on mobile, Android and iOS, from an early prototype to 100+
  B2B clients and 50,000+ users in 10+ countries. Its Android app is rated 4.7★ from 3,300+ reviews.

In each one, a single engineer was accountable for the mobile app from the first decision to the
release. That's what you should be buying.

## The cost difference: compare the total, not the rate

An agency's day rate and an independent engineer's day rate look like two prices for the same thing.
They aren't. Look at what each one pays for.

- **An agency's rate carries its overheads.** Sales, account and project managers, offices, people
  waiting between projects, and the agency's own margin are built into the rate for every developer on
  your project. You pay for them whether your app needs them or not.
- **A blended rate can hide who does the work.** Agencies often charge one rate for the whole team. If
  junior developers do most of the hours, you pay a rate set by the seniors you met for work done by the
  juniors you didn't.
- **Coordination is billable time.** Status meetings, handoffs and re-explaining your app to each new
  developer all take hours, and you pay for those hours. With one engineer, most of them disappear.
- **Rework is the most expensive line, and it's never on the quote.** A cheaper hour that produces code
  someone has to rewrite later isn't cheaper. A senior engineer's hour costs more than a junior's, but
  it's spent on the right thing the first time.
- **AI savings reach you directly.** When AI saves me time, your release comes sooner. With an agency,
  ask whether the time AI saves shows up in your estimate, or only in their margin.

The fair comparison is the **total cost of getting a working release into the store**, for the same
scope. That's hard to do when every quote is based on a different idea of what you're building. It's one
reason I start every project with a written plan: you can take it to an agency and compare quotes for
the same thing.

## AI has shifted the balance further

An agency's main advantage used to be headcount: more developers meant more code, faster. AI coding
tools have weakened that advantage. A senior engineer with AI handling the routine work, such as
boilerplate, first drafts of screens and test cases, can now own a scope that used to need a small team.

What AI hasn't made any cheaper is judgement. It can draft a screen in minutes. It can't decide what
happens when an upload fails with no signal, fill in Google Play's Data Safety form truthfully, notice
that the app runs out of memory on a cheap phone, or decide whether a release is safe to roll out. Those
calls make or break an app, and they need experience, not more people.

So the trade has changed. **More people now mostly means more code to review.** One experienced engineer
using AI well means the code gets written fast, and checked by someone who knows what to look for.

I use AI tools every day, and I review everything they produce before it goes into a client's app. Ask
anyone you're considering, agency or independent, who reviews their AI-written code, and where your code
goes when they use these tools.

## "What if you disappear?"

It's the right question to ask an independent engineer, and it deserves a real answer rather than
reassurance. Here's mine:

- **The code is yours, and you can see it from day one.** I work in my repository, give you read access
  from the start so you can see every commit, and transfer the code to yours at each paid milestone.
- **Decisions are written down**, so another developer could pick the app up later. I build apps your
  own team can take over, not ones only I understand.
- **You get working builds every week or two** on a test track, so you always know where things stand.
- **The store accounts and signing keys stay in your name.**

It's also worth asking the same question of an agency. With an agency, the risk that the person who
knows your app leaves doesn't go away. It's just less visible, because a developer can be moved to
another project without you being told.

## When I'd point you to an agency

Honestly, there's one case: **a big scope with a date that can't move**, where you need several
developers working in parallel from the first week. One engineer can't work in parallel with themselves,
however good they are.

Even then, I'd hire someone senior on your side to review what the agency builds, and make sure the
code, store accounts and signing keys are yours. A fractional lead does exactly that.

Ask these on the first call, including to me. Here are my answers:

| Who will write the code?                 | I will. The person on the call is the person building your app.              |
| Who owns the code?                       | You do. Read access from day one, transferred to you at each paid milestone. |
| When will I see the app on a real phone? | Every week or two, on a test track.                                          |
| Who handles the store release?           | I do: signing, Play's Data Safety form, and a staged rollout.                |

If an answer is vague, that tells you something too.
